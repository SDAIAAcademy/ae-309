Transfer learning

Nobody trains a vision model from nothing: start from a backbone

1 · Pretrained backbonetrained by someone else on a million or more images
→
2 · New headits last layer replaced by one for your five classes
→
3 · Fine-tunea short schedule on your own crops
What transfers: edges, textures, shapes. A network that has learnt them from a million photographs needs hundreds of road crops, not hundreds of thousands, to learn "pothole".

Participants know training loops from AE-202; the new decisions today are which backbone, how much of it to train and what it may legally be used for. The department has about 7,000 labelled training crops, far too few to train a network of eleven million weights from random values. The pilot classifier of Day 1 was itself built on a pretrained trunk; its failure was its training data, not its starting point.

Family one

A convolutional network assumes that nearby pixels belong together

Locality

Each unit looks at a small window. Edges first, then textures, then parts.

Shared weights

The same filter slides over the whole image: a crack is a crack wherever it lies.

A pyramid

Resolution falls and the view widens, layer by layer.

These assumptions are true of photographs, so the network does not have to learn them from data. That is why it does well with little.

The built-in assumptions are called inductive bias. ResNet-18, today's convolutional backbone, has 11.2 million weights in the lab and was trained on ImageNet-1k, about 1.3 million images. Its residual connections are what made deep networks trainable. The pyramid also explains what convolutional networks find hard: relating two distant parts of an image takes many layers. Sources: He et al., 2015.

Family two

A vision transformer assumes almost nothing and looks everywhere at once

1 · Patchesa 128-pixel crop is cut into 64 patches of 16 by 16
→
2 · Tokenseach patch becomes a vector, plus where it was
→
3 · Attentionevery patch can use every other patch, from the first layer
→
4 · Class tokenone summary vector feeds the head
Fewer assumptions, more data. What a convolutional network is given, a transformer must learn, so it depends more on large-scale pretraining and on a careful schedule.

ViT-Tiny, today's transformer, has 5.5 million weights in the lab and was pretrained on ImageNet-21k, about 14 million images, then on ImageNet-1k. It learnt its position embeddings for 224-pixel inputs; the lab passes img_size=128 so that the library resamples them, a detail that silently hurts accuracy when forgotten. Hybrids (hierarchical transformers, modernised convolutional networks) have blurred the line between the families; the two questions on these slides still apply to each. Sources: Dosovitskiy et al., 2020.

The interval

The bootstrap asks: how far would this number move on another sample?

def bootstrap_ci(values, n_boot=1000, seed=0):
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    idx = r.integers(0, len(values),
                     (n_boot, len(values)))
    means = values[idx].mean(axis=1)
    return (float(values.mean()),
            float(np.percentile(means, 2.5)),
            float(np.percentile(means, 97.5)))
  • values holds one 0 or 1 per test crop
  • Draw the crops again with replacement, a thousand times
  • The middle 95% of the resampled means is the interval
  • It needs no formula, so it works for any metric

The interval describes the test set, not the model: it says how much the score depends on which crops happened to be in the sample. It does not cover a second training run with another seed, which is a different source of variation. The lab's check: 80 correct of 100 gives an interval from about 0.72 to 0.88. The same eight lines return on Day 4 for the drop under rain and at night.

Reading the table

Overlap means "this test cannot tell", not "these are equal"

Intervals apart

A finding on this test set. Write it down, with the slice and its count.

Intervals overlap

No evidence either way. Collect more crops of that condition, or pair the comparison.

Never

"Equally good at night", or "better at night by 25 points". Neither is supported by 16 crops.

Slice by condition, because the release question is about conditions. Slicing by class answers another useful question: which defect is hard.

Comparing two separate intervals is the cautious test: intervals can overlap a little while the paired difference is clearly above zero. So the rule in practice: when intervals overlap and the decision matters, bootstrap the difference on paired crops before collecting more data. Results by lighting, weather and district are also the course's responsible-use commitment: no part of the city is served worse unnoticed.

Session 2

How much to fine-tune

  • Train a linear probe on frozen features
  • Compare a probe with full fine-tuning at two label budgets
  • Decide whether more labels or more training is the next step
02
How much to train

Fine-tuning has three depths

Linear probe

The backbone is frozen. Only the new last layer is trained: 2,565 weights on a ResNet-18.

Partial fine-tuning

The last block and the head are trained. The early layers, which hold edges and textures, stay as they are.

Full fine-tuning

Every weight moves: 11.2 million on a ResNet-18.

The deeper you train, the more labels and care it takes. Millions of weights and a few hundred labels can overfit, or forget what pretraining taught.

The probe's 2,565 weights are 512 features times five classes plus five biases. Between the three depths lie more options: a lower learning rate for the backbone than for the head, unfreezing layer by layer, and small adapter modules inside a frozen backbone. The same question governs all of them: how many weights can my labels support? The stretch item of the lab trains the last block only and places it in the table.

The probe

A probe runs the backbone once and trains on what comes out

def frozen_features(name, X):
    model = U.backbone(name)
    if name == "resnet18":
        model[1].fc = nn.Identity()
    else:
        model[1].head = nn.Identity()
    return U.predict(model, X, size=128)

def linear_probe(F_train, y_train, F_test):
    mu, sd = F_train.mean(0), F_train.std(0) + 1e-6
    clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000)
    clf.fit((F_train - mu) / sd, y_train)
    return clf.predict((F_test - mu) / sd)
  • A frozen backbone gives the same output for a crop every time: compute it once
  • The classifier is a logistic regression on 512 or 192 numbers per crop
  • Under half a minute on a laptop for 717 crops
  • It measures how much of the task the pretrained features already solve

The probe is the cheapest experiment in transfer learning and should be the first one run on any new task. Standardising the features with the training mean and standard deviation matters for the regression to converge. The stored features can be reused for every later experiment on a frozen backbone: other classifiers, other label budgets, a search for mislabelled crops.

The table answers the budget question: label more, or train more?

The answer belongs to this data. On another task ten times the labels may be worth twenty points. The method transfers; the conclusion does not.

The lab's rule of thumb compares two gains on the best backbone: from the probe to full fine-tuning at 10% of the labels, and from 10% to 100% under full fine-tuning. Here 0.163 against 0.014. The synthetic defect classes are easier than real road damage, which is one reason so few labels go so far; say it. What the table cannot tell is the cost of a label: Sultan Al-Yami's inspectors label crops between their inspections, and every hour of labelling is an hour off the road.

Choosing a depth

Start shallow, and go deeper only when the table says so

You haveStart withBecause
Tens of labels per classA linear probeA few thousand weights cannot overfit much, and it runs in seconds
Hundreds per class, a task close to photographsThe last block and the headEarly features already fit; fewer weights to move
Hundreds per class, pictures unlike the pretraining dataFull fine-tuning at a low rateThe features themselves must change: night, dust, a view from above
A probe that beats full fine-tuning is a warning about the fine-tuning, not a miracle: too few labels, too high a rate, or too many passes.

Road crops seen from straight above, in dust or at night, are unlike ImageNet's photographs, which is why full fine-tuning pays here even on 717 labels. Practical care when every weight moves: a learning rate ten or more times lower than for training from scratch, a warm-up, and watching the validation slices for the pass at which they stop improving. U.fit in roadkit is the plain loop participants know, with AdamW and a one-cycle schedule.

What does this result tell you?

On 150 labelled crops a linear probe scores 79%. Full fine-tuning of the same backbone on the same crops, for 30 passes at a from-scratch learning rate, scores 71%. What is the likely explanation?

03
Open-vocabulary models

An image-text model puts pictures and sentences in one space

How it was trained

On hundreds of millions of pictures with their captions: an image tower and a text tower learn to give a picture and its own caption nearby vectors.

What that allows

Describe a class in words, and the model can score any picture against the description. No task labels, no training.

Open vocabulary: the classes are not fixed when the model is trained. You name them when you use it.

The model in the lab is CLIP with a ViT-B/32 image tower, loaded through OpenCLIP: about 600 MB, and it runs on a laptop processor because nothing is trained. Radford et al. report 400 million image-text pairs for its training. This session uses such a model for one purpose: a baseline. Vision-language models as systems (questions about images, grounding, multimodal applications) are the subject of AE-408. Sources: Radford et al., 2021; the OpenCLIP repository.

Zero-shot classification

The classifier is a list of sentences

@torch.no_grad()
def zero_shot(prompts, image_emb):
    t = clip.encode_text(
        tokenizer(prompts).to(U.DEV)).float()
    t = (t / t.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)).cpu()
    return (image_emb @ t.T).argmax(dim=1).numpy()
  1. Write one sentence per class
  2. Embed the sentences and the crops, scale each vector to unit length
  3. Similarity is a matrix product
  4. Each crop gets the class of its most similar sentence

Six lines, and no label was used. The image embeddings are computed once, like the probe's features, so trying another set of prompts costs a fraction of a second. That makes prompt wording an experiment anyone can run, and also a way to tune on the test set without noticing: choose prompts on validation crops, report on test crops.

Using the floor

A floor tells you what your labels were worth

Far above the floor

Rasid: 94% against 51%. The labels and the training earned their cost.

Just above it

A few points for thousands of labels: ask whether the simple baseline, with no training to maintain, is the better system.

Below a constant answer

The zero-shot model is no classifier for this task. Say so and move on.

The same model can still help with the data: searching frames by a sentence, proposing labels for a person to confirm, a few-shot classifier from five labelled crops per class.

The stretch item builds the few-shot variant: average the image embeddings of five training crops per class and classify by the nearest average, 25 labels and no training. Licences of open-weight backbones

04
Licences

"Open" is not one thing: code, weights and data each carry terms

The code

What builds the network. Usually under a permissive software licence.

The weights

What runs on the roadside unit. Licensed separately by whoever trained them, and often differently.

The training data

Its terms of access may reach the weights trained on it. The record names it for the legal officer.

The library's licence says nothing about a model's weights. One library ships hundreds of sets of weights under a dozen different terms.

The usual mistake is to read the licence file of the code repository and stop. What a municipality deploys is the weights. torchvision's documentation reminds users that its pretrained models may carry terms derived from the data they were trained on. Engineers do not settle legal questions; they make sure the questions are asked before the training budget is spent, with the facts collected. That is the lab. Sources: the torchvision documentation on models and pretrained weights.

Two traps

Terms travel with the weights, and libraries have licences too

Fine-tuning does not wash a licence

Weights fine-tuned from non-commercial weights are derived from them. Do not assume they are yours to deploy: choose the starting weights before any training.

The detection library

Ultralytics publishes its models and code under AGPL-3.0, with an enterprise licence as the alternative. Under the department's rule: a commercial licence, or another library.

Run the licence check before the first experiment, not after the best model has been found.

The second card is the stretch item: run a record for the detection library through the same rule. Tool names in this course are examples of a capability, not endorsements; a detection library with network copyleft terms is a normal thing to meet, and both ways forward are legitimate. Other things that carry terms and get forgotten: the labelling tool's export, a public dataset used for pretraining, an image-text model used to propose labels. Sources: the Ultralytics licensing page.
